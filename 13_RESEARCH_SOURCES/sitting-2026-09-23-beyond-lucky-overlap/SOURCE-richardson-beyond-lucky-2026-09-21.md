Author: Anthony Richardson (https://au.linkedin.com/in/anthonypr) — Aurecon / Greater Brisbane Area; Pulse byline claims Mt Isa upbringing and 35 years in resources.

---

What we could leave the next generation, if resources, defence and compute became more than something we host.

Donald Horne's "The Lucky Country" was never meant as a compliment. He meant a nation that succeeded despite itself, riding second-rate leadership and first-rate luck. Sixty years on, we've built an entire economic identity on proving him right: dig it up, pump it out, ship it away, and let the rest of the world capture the value we could have left for the generation after us.

I grew up in Mt Isa, where you felt the earth shake under your feet twice a day like clockwork, the blast schedule was just part of how time passed. It was a time when the sporting clubs were a plenty, the rodeo became legendary, and the whole town felt like it belonged to itself, built by the people who worked the mine, not owned from somewhere else. I've spent 35 years in the resources sector since, and I've watched Australia do "lucky" better than almost anyone on earth. What I want to see now is us do "legacy" just as well: build something worth handing to the generation after us, not just something worth extracting today.

There's a live conversation right now about building the next phase of the economy on three pillars: Natural Resources, Space/Defence, and Compute. When I say resources, I mean the full base, minerals, gas, solar, water, the whole endowment, not just what comes out of a pit. This is a genuinely exciting moment. We have real comparative advantage, real geopolitical tailwind, and real capital flowing in, all at once. The opportunity in front of us is bigger than any single mining boom we've ridden before.

To be clear, this isn't a criticism of the leaders and organisations who've built these sectors into what they are today. Our resources companies, our defence institutions, our researchers are genuinely world class, and they've delivered decades of prosperity most nations would envy. This is about something additive: what we choose to leave the generation after us. The structures that got us here were built for a different era's opportunity, and they built us the platform to now leave a bigger one.

## The opportunity in front of us

Today, we hold some of the best mineral, gas, solar and water endowments on the planet, and much of the equipment, extraction technology and processing IP running them is licensed in from overseas. We're about to make the largest peacetime defence investment in our history, with the chance to negotiate real IP ownership into how that money is spent. We're becoming one of the world's most attractive places to build a data centre, thanks to our energy and stability. The next step is making sure the AI running inside it, and the value it creates, is something we can hand on rather than something we merely rent.

Three sectors, one pattern worth changing: Australia has always supplied the land, the capital risk and the raw input brilliantly. The next chapter is about keeping more of the compounding asset ourselves, so there's something of real weight to leave behind.

## What a real inheritance could look like

It's not a slogan swap. A resource fortune spent as it comes in is a boom. A resource fortune converted into institutions, skills and technology that keep generating income long after the deposit is worked out is an inheritance. The difference isn't sentiment, it's structure: who owns the platform once the royalty cheque stops.

Resources becomes a technology story as much as a tonnage and megalitre one: Australian-owned autonomous extraction and operational-AI platforms with scale, sovereign processing capability, and solar and water technology exported rather than imported. We already have more mining equipment, technology and services firms than almost any resource economy on earth, and a genuine renewables and water-engineering base to match. What we're missing is a mechanism, a sovereign fund, a technology-licensing structure, whatever the model, that turns today's royalties into tomorrow's owned IP, rather than letting them disappear into a single year's budget.

The METS sector alone employs tens of thousands of Australians in engineering, software and advanced manufacturing roles that barely existed a generation ago. Scaled and capitalised properly, that's not a services industry propping up mining, it's a technology export industry in its own right, the kind that still exists and still pays wages long after any single orebody is exhausted.

Defence and space start operating like DARPA or Israel's Unit 8200: government spend as de-risking demand for Australian-owned IP, with a clear path toward commercial independence rather than an open-ended subsidy for hardware built somewhere else. The real test of legacy here isn't the hardware delivered, it's where the people go afterward. Unit 8200 alumni didn't just finish their service and move to the next posting; they went on to found much of Israel's cybersecurity industry. If our defence and space programs are going to leave anything behind, it has to be the same thing: engineers and founders who take what they built in uniform and build companies Australia owns with it.

Compute becomes the applied layer sitting across the other two: Australian-owned AI for resource discovery and management, autonomous logistics, defence systems, and grid and water modelling. Cheap energy is our access point, and it can become our product too, the platforms and models trained on Australian problems, owned by Australian entities, licensed back out to the world instead of the other way around.

None of these industries stand alone. Resources supply the problems and the capital base, compute supplies the tools to solve them at scale, and defence supplies demanding early customers and a talent pipeline that can move across all three. Built separately, they're three industries competing for the same engineers. Built as one system, they're the beginning of an economy that still owns something a generation from now, not just an economy that once hosted a boom.

## What it will take

None of this happens on current settings, and it's worth being honest about that rather than waving it away. But the things standing in the way are all within our control, not accidents of geography or timing.

1. Capital that matches the ambition. We sit on one of the largest superannuation pools on earth, and the overwhelming majority of it flows into listed equities and offshore assets, not the domestic ventures that would actually own the next generation of Australian IP. Norway didn't build its sovereign fund by hoping oil companies reinvested generously, it legislated where the money went. We don't need to copy that model exactly, but we do need the equivalent instinct: a deliberate mechanism, whether that's a cornerstone co-investment vehicle, a mandated allocation, or a dedicated deep-tech fund, that turns resource and defence dollars into equity in the ventures building the IP, not just contracts for services rendered.

2. A talent strategy that multiplies rather than cannibalises. Right now, resources, defence and compute would all recruit from the same narrow pool of engineers and bid each other's wages up while the total number of people entering these fields barely moves. The fix isn't just more STEM funding, it's building a workforce that moves between the three deliberately: mid-career resources engineers rotating into applied AI, defence programs graduating founders instead of just returning them to civilian postings, and university and TAFE pipelines built around all three sectors as one talent market rather than three competing ones.

3. Different metrics. Tonnes shipped, megalitres delivered and program dollars spent measure activity. They tell you nothing about whether ownership is actually accumulating in Australian hands. Every government-funded program in these three sectors should be reporting the numbers that matter for an inheritance: IP registered and retained onshore, ventures spun out, Australian equity retained through their growth, and product sold offshore under an Australian licence. If we don't measure it, we won't manage it, and we definitely won't notice if twenty years of spending quietly financed someone else's balance sheet instead of ours.

4. Institutional patience that survives a change of government. A twenty-to-thirty year build doesn't work if the settings reset every election cycle, and we've already seen defence funding shift between roughly 2 and 3 per cent of GDP depending on who's in office. Israel's cyber sector and Finland's tech pivot both took decades of continuity that outlasted individual governments. Whatever structure we choose, it needs political capital with real vision for legacy that's different to the vision of those before them, a vision that can create the mandate that sits above the ordinary budget cycle, or none of the other three points get the time they need to compound.

## A legacy worth building

I still think about that town sometimes, the rodeo dust, the mine whistle marking the day, the sense that everything around us had been built by people who intended to stay. That's what a legacy actually feels like from the inside, not a line in a budget paper, but a place that belongs to the people who built it. I'd like the generation after us to be able to say the same thing about this whole country, not just about one town in it.

The country that gets this right doesn't stop being lucky. It leaves its next generation something better than luck: an inheritance that's strategic, sovereign, owned. It's a twenty to thirty year build, closer to Finland's post Nokia pivot or Israel's cyber transition than anything in a single budget cycle, but it's a build we are genuinely well placed to make. Few nations start this race with our endowment, our institutions and our talent already in place.

We've had the luck. Now we get to build something worth inheriting, if we are brave enough.
