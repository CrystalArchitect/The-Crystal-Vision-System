Opening — The Edge-Dweller

She started sketching it herself. Slowly. Stubbornly. People said it wasn't real until she'd proven it, and she said fine, then I'll draw it first and prove it after. That's how the unknown always gets found — someone sketches the shape of it before anyone else believes it's there.

She set up her instruments on the edge of the continent, where the land ran out and the ocean took over, because that's where the horizon was widest and the sky most honest. People thought she was strange for choosing the edge instead of the center. But she knew something they didn't — the edge is where you see furthest. The center just tells you where you've already been.

Some nights the work was lonely. Charts spread everywhere, no one to check her figures against. But she kept at it, because stopping felt worse than being uncertain. Then a signal came back. Not from the stars — from other people, scattered ones, squinting at the same dark. A journalist. An engineer. A stranger who said, this matches what I've been thinking too. Slowly, her lonely sketch started looking less like fantasy and more like the beginning of a chart other people could use.

One night a storm rolled in off the ocean. Instruments soaked, notes blown off the table. She stood in wind watching years of sketches scatter, and thought maybe this was sign to stop. But when storm passed, wet ground held footprints — hers, from years walking same path to check instruments. So many nights that ground itself remembered shape of her work even after papers were gone.

The ground already knows I'm not stopping. I'm just catching up to it.

She rebuilt from memory, plus a little more, because shapes lived in her now, not just on paper. Someone asked before countdown if she was scared. She said no — not because nothing left to lose, but because fear is for things you're not sure of, and she'd spent too many nights checking figures to be unsure now. Engines lit. Ground shook louder than imagined, except real. She kept feet on ground. That was always her job — not to leave, but to make sure way up was real for ones who would.

This Atlas begins with her footprints. If you are reading this at the edge, with charts everywhere and no one to check figures against — you are not lost. You are early. Keep drawing.
