# Australian Creators Whose Work Was Scraped for AI Training and the Proposed Training Data Discoverability Obligations

## Executive Summary

Australian creators across literature, music, and visual arts have publicly confirmed that their works were included in large-scale AI training datasets without permission, compensation, or attribution. The Books3 corpus, comprising 183,000 books, and music datasets such as Sleeping-DISCO-9M and LAION-DISCO-12M, containing up to 12.3 million tracks, have been identified as sources. In response, the Australian Government in October 2025 rejected a text and data mining (TDM) exception that would have legalised such use, instead establishing a Copyright and AI Reference Group (CAIRG) to develop a paid collective licensing framework and mandatory transparency obligations. The proposed discoverability framework, articulated in submissions to the Productivity Commission and Senate inquiries, would require AI developers operating in Australia to maintain comprehensive records of training data sources and implement searchable databases allowing copyright holders to determine if their works were used.

## 3. Exact Wording of Proposed Training Data Discoverability Obligations

Australia does not yet have a statutory discoverability obligation in force. The wording being advocated comes from three sources: submissions to the Productivity Commission and Senate Select Committee, and the EU model that Australian industry groups have endorsed.

### 3.1 Australian Creator Groups' Proposed Mandatory Disclosure Framework

In a submission to the Productivity Commission on Harnessing Data and Digital Technology, Nik Samoylov proposed the clearest operational wording, which has been cited by creator groups:

> "Under the penalty of statutory damages, require all generative AI model developers and providers operating in Australia to:
> - Maintain comprehensive records of all training data sources
> - Implement searchable databases allowing copyright holders to determine if their works were used" [[13]](https://engage.pc.gov.au/document/806)

This requirement would apply:

> "not only to those who make their models available through chatbots and API to third parties, but also to those who train generative AI above a certain size (e.g. more than 1 terabyte of data) because they can then use the trained models to compete with copyright holders" [[13]](https://engage.pc.gov.au/document/806)

The Australian Society of Authors made a parallel demand:

> "Oblige all companies which offer generative AI to be transparent about their training datasets. The copyright works they have 'ingested' must be disclosed. Creators are entitled to understand how and to what extent their works have been, and will be, used, and for what purpose." [[14]](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/submissions/82615/0035%20Australian%20Society%20of%20Authors.pdf)

And more broadly:

> "Establishing an obligation for the platforms that operate in Australia to make their so-called training data discoverable, at a minimum, would be really helpful" [[15]](https://aapnews.aap.com.au/news/call-to-tax-companies-that-replace-workers-with-ai)

### 3.2 APRA AMCOS Endorsement of EU Model

APRA AMCOS's submission to the Senate Select Committee on Adopting AI explicitly endorses:

> "APRA AMCOS endorses the recommendation by CISAC that the government put in place obligations on AI developers to disclose their training data to public authorities, with reference to provisions included in the EU AI Act (Art 53(1)(d)) and the proposed 'Generative AI Copyright Disclosure Act' in the United States." [[16]](https://www.aph.gov.au/DocumentStore.ashx?id=64e1ce90-7df1-4f3f-8af9-8dbcc69e7e9b&subId=757063)[[17]](https://assets.apraamcos.com.au/images/PDFs/About/APRA-AMCOS-submissions_Senate-Inquiry-AI_22052024.pdf)

### 3.3 EU AI Act Article 53(1)(d) – The Reference Standard

The EU provision being referenced provides:

> "Article 53(1)(d) EU AI Act requires GPAI providers to publish a 'sufficiently detailed summary' of the training data used for their models." [[18]](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/taking-the-eu-ai-act-to-practice-decoding-the-gpai-code-of-practice-and-the-training-data-summary-te)

The Commission's Template operationalises this as requiring disclosure of:

> "general information about training modalities and data size, identify large public datasets, and provide narrative descriptions of licensed and private data sources, scraped content" [[18]](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/taking-the-eu-ai-act-to-practice-decoding-the-gpai-code-of-practice-and-the-training-data-summary-te)

And more fully:

> "general information about training modalities and data size, identify large public datasets, and provide narrative descriptions of licensed and private data sources, scraped content (including the most relevant domains), user data, and synthetic data." [[19]](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/taking-the-eu-ai-act-to-practice-decoding-the-gpai-code-of-practice-and-the-training-data-summary-te)

The Template is designed to avoid work-by-work disclosure but requires aggregated reporting and narrative explanations.

### 3.4 Australian Government's Current Policy Direction

Following rejection of the TDM exception in October 2025, the Government convened the Copyright and AI Reference Group (CAIRG) to discuss:

> "whether a new paid collective licensing framework under the Copyright Act should be established for AI, or whether to maintain the status quo through a voluntary licensing framework." [[20]](https://artsreview.com.au/federal-government-to-ensure-australia-is-prepared-for-future-copyright-challenges-emerging-from-ai/)

And:

> "Encourage fair, legal avenues for using copyright material in AI – Examining whether a new paid collective licensing framework under the Copyright Act should be established for AI" [[20]](https://artsreview.com.au/federal-government-to-ensure-australia-is-prepared-for-future-copyright-challenges-emerging-from-ai/)

Prime Minister Albanese announced establishment of an Office of AI within the Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet to coordinate this, stating:

> "No company should use Australian books, music, art, or news to build or train AI without the artist's control, and that includes the artist's control of the price and value of their work." [[21]](https://if.com.au/anything-less-is-theft-prime-minister-to-establish-office-of-ai-promises-protection-for-artists-in-new-laws/)

## 4. Compensation Mechanisms and Precedents

### 4.1 US Litigation Precedent

In September 2025, Anthropic agreed to a $1.5 billion settlement over use of pirated books:

> "A federal judge has approved a $1.5 billion copyright settlement in which artificial intelligence company Anthropic will pay thousands of authors about $3,000 per book after using pirated copies of their works to train its Claude chatbot." [[22]](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/post/judge-approves-1-5b-anthropic-settlement-over-pirated-books-used-to-train-chatbot/)

The lawsuit alleged Anthropic pulled works from shadow libraries like Library Genesis, Books3, and Pirate Library Mirror [[23]](https://www.eweek.com/news/judge-approves-anthropic-settlement-authors-copyright/).

### 4.2 Proposed Australian Licensing Models

Creator groups propose three pillars:

1. **Strengthened enforcement** – fast-track court procedures, statutory damages reflecting commercial scale, reverse burden of proof where defendants cannot demonstrate licensing compliance [[13]](https://engage.pc.gov.au/document/806)
2. **Mandatory disclosure** – searchable databases as above
3. **Market-led voluntary opt-in licensing** – voluntary and market-based, never compulsory; opt-in not opt-out; respectful of creators' right to refuse; transparent terms [[13]](https://engage.pc.gov.au/document/806)

APRA AMCOS's AI and Music Report estimated that without mandatory licensing, songwriters in Australia and New Zealand could lose 23 per cent of income from music rights, projecting more than $500 million in lost revenue over four years [[10]](https://www.noise11.com/news/apra-amcos-australian-new-zealand-songs-ai-training-datasets-20260619).

### 4.3 Transparency Demands

Industry surveys show:

> "97% demand that AI providers should be obliged to disclose when they use copyrighted works as training data." [[24]](https://assets.apraamcos.co.nz/images/PDFs/AI-and-Music-Survey-Results-Press-Release.pdf)

## Conclusion

The list of Australian creators who have publicly confirmed scraping extends to at least 18,000 titles by 15,000 authors and thousands of songs across genres, with Indigenous works raising additional cultural risk concerns. The discoverability obligation being sought is not yet law in Australia but has precise proposed wording: maintain comprehensive records and implement searchable databases under penalty of statutory damages, modeled on EU AI Act Article 53(1)(d) which requires a sufficiently detailed summary of training content including large public datasets, licensed sources, and scraped domains. The Government's October 2025 decision to reject a TDM exception and explore a paid collective licensing framework via CAIRG and the new Office of AI represents the current policy pathway toward compensation.
