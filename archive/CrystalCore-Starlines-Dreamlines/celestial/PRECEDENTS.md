# Precedents

Historical artefacts this layer learns from. Each one is here because it
demonstrates something the Incognita Rule asserts, rather than because it
looks like the wheel.

> Always mark which lines are dreamed and which are surveyed, and never let a
> dreamed line pretend it was measured.

## Apian's spheres, 1524 — precision is not provenance

**Belt: Science** (the publication history below is sourced and checkable).
**What it demonstrates: how a dreamed line acquires a surveyed coat.**

Peter Apian — Petrus Apianus, born Peter Bienewitz, 1495–1552, of Leisnig in
Saxony — published the first edition of his cosmography in 1524. It contains a
diagram of the universe as ten nested crystalline spheres: the four elements
at the centre, then Luna, Mercury, Venus, Sol, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the
firmament of fixed stars, the Ninth and Tenth Heavens, the *Primum Mobile*,
and outside all motion the **Empyrean** — *habitacvlvm dei et omnivm
electorvm*, the habitation of God and all the Elect.

Every sphere in it is wrong. Not one of them exists.

That is not the interesting part. The interesting part is that the diagram is
**careful**. Apian was a working mathematician who was appointed Professor of
Mathematics at Ingolstadt in 1527, became court mathematician to Charles V,
and produced in the *Astronomicum Caesareum* (1540) instrument diagrams of
genuine computational precision. His cosmography ran to more than thirty
reprintings in fourteen languages and remained the basis of mathematical
geography for roughly a century. Copernicus published *De revolutionibus
orbium coelestium* nineteen years after the first edition, and the spheres
outlived him in print regardless.

So the artefact is a model that was:

- drawn to scale, with internal consistency
- produced by a competent specialist
- grounded in a long-established body of thought (Aristotle, Ptolemy)
- reproduced, translated and taught for a century
- **entirely unmeasured**, in the part that mattered most

A modern reader is tempted to file this under superstition. It is not
superstition; it is the failure mode this project is named for, committed by
someone doing careful work. The Empyrean was drawn in the same ink, at the
same line weight, with the same confident lettering, as the orbit of the Moon.
Nothing on the plate distinguishes the sphere anybody could check from the
sphere nobody could.

**That is the whole rule, in one woodcut.** It is why `star()` will not accept
a position without a catalogue and epoch, why `correspondence()` and
`reading()` refuse to be filed as surveyed, and why the drawn wheel carries
*dreamed line — not an astronomical chart* on its own face rather than in a
caption that can be cropped away.

### Two corrections to the caption it was read from

The plate came to this project as a photograph of a page in a modern popular
book, whose caption reads *"Apian's Cosmographia of 1524"*. That compresses
two things, and this section exists partly to demonstrate the compression —
the same discipline, applied to the source that taught it.

**The 1524 title was not *Cosmographia*.** The first edition, published by
Johann Weyssenburger at Landshut, was ***Cosmographicus liber***. Gemma
Frisius — Apian's Dutch student — later edited and expanded the work under the
simpler title *Cosmographia*, the form that became the bestseller. The Antwerp
edition of 1533 incorporates Frisius's triangulation method, which is not
Apian's. A citation reading "Apian, *Cosmographia*, 1524" therefore names a
title that did not yet exist and silently credits one author for two people's
work.

**The reproduced plate is not from the 1524 Latin edition.** Its captions are
in Spanish — *Figura de la diuision de las Spheras*; *de la Cosmographia. Fo.
3.*; *decimo cielo pone los Theologos el cielo empireo, adonde habita dios con
los bienauenturados* — so it comes from one of the vernacular translations,
decades later, not from the first edition the caption dates it to. (Antwerp
produced a Spanish *Libro de la Cosmographia* in 1548; whether this particular
plate is that edition is **not** established here, and is not claimed.)

Neither is a scandal. Both are ordinary editorial compression in a book
written for general readers, and the book is a good one. They are recorded
because a compression is how a provenance goes missing — not by anyone lying,
but by a date and a title travelling together until they look like one fact.
Which is precisely what happened to the spheres.

## The word *atlas*, 1595 — a provenance overwritten by a picture

**Belt: Science** (Mercator's own preface; checkable).
**What it demonstrates: how a stated source is lost without anyone lying.**

Everyone knows the word *atlas* comes from the Titan condemned to hold up the
sky. Mercator, who coined the usage, **said in his own preface that he meant
somebody else.**

He named the collection for **Atlas, King of Mauretania** — a legendary
philosopher, mathematician and astronomer to whom the making of the first
celestial globe was attributed. That Atlas is the one on the frontispiece of
the 1595 edition. Later editions changed the frontispiece to the Titan, and
the Titan is now the near-universal gloss.

Nobody falsified anything. An illustration was swapped, the new illustration
travelled with the word, and four centuries later the stated provenance is the
minority reading of a term used daily by cartographers. **A picture outran a
preface.**

That is the same mechanism as the Apian entry above, seen from the other side.
Apian shows an unmeasured claim drawn in measured ink; this shows a documented
one erased by a decorative choice. Both are reasons the drawn wheel carries its
belt on its own face rather than in a caption, and reasons `star()` demands a
catalogue and epoch rather than a plausible number: **the label has to travel
with the thing, because the thing will outlive the context it was made in.**

Two smaller corrections to the popular account, while the record is open:
publication of Mercator's collection began in **1585** and was completed
posthumously in 1595, and Ortelius's *Theatrum Orbis Terrarum* (1570) is
generally called the first modern atlas. Mercator's contribution was the
*name*, not the format.

Worth noting for this project specifically: the man Mercator actually honoured
was a **celestial** cartographer. For a layer that puts a sky over a map, the
true etymology fits better than the myth that replaced it.

## Not yet entered

**A hand-drawn cycle chart**, twenty-nine spokes for a lunar cycle, each wedge
holding what was dreamt or what happened that day, with empty days written in
as `void` rather than left blank. Structurally it is a different atlas from
this one — days around a cycle, where ours is works around a tonal wheel — and
it arrives at the same discipline independently: *a marked absence is a
reading; an unmarked one is silence.*

It is **not implemented**, and the reason is attribution rather than
difficulty. It is a named living author's published method, reproduced as a
template in her book. Implementing the structure while dropping the name is
not a fix — it removes the acknowledgment, not the borrowing.

**The citation is confirmed from the object.**

> Connie Cockrell Kaplan, *The Woman's Book of Dreams: Dreaming as a Spiritual
> Practice*, foreword by Jamie Sams. © 1999 by Connie Cockrell Kaplan; Beyond
> Words Publishing, Inc., Hillsboro, Oregon. **This edition printed for Axiom
> Publishing, 2000.** ISBN 1-86476-056-7 (pbk.). LCCN 98-50197.

Read off a photograph of the cover, held by the same person who photographed
page 63 — the *Dream Chart*: a twenty-nine-spoke wheel filled in by hand, blank
template facing, Name field *Connie Kaplan*, dates 29 December 1994 to 26
January 1995, captioned "(29-day cycle)". Several wedges are written `void`.

**And the copyright page corrected the one field that had been flagged as
weaker than the rest.** The ISBN carried here from catalogue records was
`1-58270-008-7` — the Beyond Words printing. The copy the chart came from is
an Australian reprint: *This Edition Printed For Axiom Publishing, 2000*, ISBN
`1-86476-056-7`. Both ISBNs are real. They are not the same book in hand.

That is the entire argument for splitting a citation by how each part is known.
The field marked *not from the object* was the field that was wrong, and it was
wrong in the quietest way available — a correct number for a different
printing, which no amount of re-reading the sentence would catch. `CITATION`
keeps the superseded ISBN rather than deleting it, so the correction stays
legible.

One assumption remains, unchanged by any of the three photographs: that page
63, the cover and the copyright page are the same copy.

**An earlier version of this entry named this book as fact before anyone had
looked.** It was an inference from a search match with its inferential step
removed — the same error this file records two sections above against a modern
book's Apian caption. The guess turned out right. That changes nothing about it
having been a guess wearing the clothes of a fact, and it is recorded here
rather than quietly overwritten, because a correction that erases its own
occasion teaches nobody anything.

**A note on lineage.** The book's foreword is by Jamie Sams, co-author of
*Medicine Cards*, and the worked example includes a wedge reading "ceremony
with BIA". The book therefore sits adjacent to material this project holds a
hard boundary around. What has been implemented is the calendrical form alone —
twenty-nine wedges, a mark per day, and the distinction between a day written
empty and a day nobody wrote in. No cultural content, teaching or practice from
the book is reproduced, and none should be added without the consent the
governance document requires. The adjacency is recorded because noticing it is
the discipline; it is not a claim that the diagram carries restriction.

Structurally it is a different atlas from the wheel: days around a cycle rather
than works around a tonal ring. It does not subsume, and is not subsumed by,
what is built here.
