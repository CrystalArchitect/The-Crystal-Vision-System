## 2. Dharawal cosmology (public layers)

Country is living and continuous with Sky Country. Spirits that shaped the world are understood to have come from the sky. Land, sea, sky, animals, plants, people and spirits of the dead form one field.

### Biame (Baiame / Biamie)

Sky creator spirit in many south-east accounts, including Dharawal. Came down to what was formless, shaped the land, set laws of life, returned to the sky as Sky Hero. Other ancestral beings, including a creation serpent, finished parts of the work.

### Other public elements

- Spirit ancestors created Country and life forms; this shaped kinship, social structures and lore.
- Spirits of the dead can return in low-lying cloud. Some Dharawal people later read the 1770 Endeavour sails through that lens.
- Aunty Fran Bodkin’s D’harawal stories and seasonal calendar are a major accessible public record: plants, animals, seasons, place.
- Sydney Basin / Thurrawal–Dharawal sky notes: Pleiades (Seven Sisters) linked in some accounts to spring, plant flowering and Wagalag Sisters Dreaming; Orion’s Belt associated with summer.
- Broader Australian Seven Sisters Songlines are ancient and widespread. Local Dharawal public record of the sisters is present but less fully elaborated than some desert Songlines.
- Deeper, restricted or gendered knowledge is not public and belongs with knowledge holders.

Sources used in session included National Museum of Australia (La Perouse collaboration), dharawalstories.com (Frances Bodkin), ABC Deep Time / Raymond Ingrey, Bursill et al. Dharawal book, and Sydney Basin astronomy papers.

## 3. Sydney Basin rock engravings

One of the densest Aboriginal petroglyph concentrations in Australia. Thousands of sites on Hawkesbury sandstone. Most locations unpublished to prevent damage and because they remain sacred.

- Style: Simple Figurative — outline figures, pecked then often abraded.
- Age: style generally associated with the last ~5,000 years. Occupation of the region goes back much further.
- Common motifs: whales, fish, sharks, kangaroos/wallabies, birds, human footprints (mundoes), shields, weapons.
- Culture-hero figures: Baiame-type (frontal, often large, sometimes headdress/objects); Daramulan-type (profile, sometimes one large foot or bird-like features, often with emu). These appear more densely north of the Georges River but ancestral imagery is recognised across the Basin, including Dharawal Country.
- Placement: elevated sandstone platforms, often ridgelines with views.

### Public Dharawal site: Jibbon Headland (Djeeban)

Royal National Park, near Bundeena. Most extensive engraving complex in the park. Now viewed from a boardwalk/platform built with Dharawal Local Aboriginal Land Council to stop foot-traffic damage. Figures include large whales (including overlapping orcas), stingray, turtle, wallaby/kangaroo, and a human/ancestral figure. Whale is a major coastal Dharawal totem. Interpretive material developed with community links some figures to hunting success, stranding, and ancestral presence.

Les Bursill and collaborating Dharawal knowledge holders documented many southern sites and noted Biame as sky spirit, with possible related engravings including references around Heathcote.

## 4. Dharawal whale vessel story (Burriburri) — EXTERNAL public telling

Public tellings carried by Raymond Ingrey (La Perouse / Gujaga Foundation) and related Illawarra / La Perouse community sources. Also summarised in National Museum of Australia Dharawal Dreaming material made with La Perouse community. Oral stories vary in names and detail; this is a composite of the public versions, not a closed official text.

### Story spine (AI composite of public versions — NOT Crystal authorship; NOT restricted text)

Long ago the old people lived in human form in a land far to the east (*ngarawan*). Country there became barren. They needed to travel west. Small canoes (*madjuri*) could not make the crossing.

Only *Burriburri* (Whale), a large and greedy man, had a vessel big enough (*barangga*). He would not share.

The people asked his friend *Gunaagan* (Starfish; some tellings: Junagen) to distract him — often by looking through his hair for lice. Burriburri pulled the vessel into the shallows and lay down. The people boarded and rowed away.

Burriburri discovered the theft, fought Gunaagan, and was wounded in the top of the head. Starfish fell to the sea floor and became starfish. Burriburri swam after the vessel and transformed into the humpback whale — the wound becoming the blowhole (first blood, then water).

On the vessel others transformed: koala grew fur, arms and ears while rowing; crane (*Galu*) danced at the sight of land, grew feathers and a beak, and long legs punched holes in the vessel.

They reached land. The people — now animals — climbed out. Koala pushed the vessel back to sea. It turned over and became *Gangmangang* (Windang Island) off Lake Illawarra.

Burriburri and descendants still travel the coast looking for the lost vessel. Harbours, bays and islands from Sydney Harbour to the Shoalhaven mark where they circled. Whales still have lice. The blowhole remains.

### What it carries

- Origin of certain animals on Country
- Creation of specific coastal landforms
- Ongoing totemic presence of the whale
- Living relation to whale engravings (including Jibbon) and to seasonal whale movement along the east coast

A bronze *Burri Burri* sculpture at Lake Illawarra (Reddall Reserve) was commissioned as a public artwork grounded in this story / Gun-man-gang connection.
