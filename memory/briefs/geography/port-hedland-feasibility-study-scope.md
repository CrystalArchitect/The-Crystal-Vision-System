# Port Hedland Phase 1 — Feasibility Study Scope

Terms of Reference for an Independent Study · TerAustralis Incognita · May 2026

## 1. Purpose of this Document

This document is not a feasibility study. It is a scope — a set of terms of reference — setting out what an independent feasibility study of a proposed Phase 1 hub at Port Hedland would need to examine. Its purpose is to help commission, request or fund such a study.

A genuine feasibility study must be carried out by qualified, independent specialists with access to site data. This document defines the task for them; it does not pre-empt their findings.

## 2. Background

TerAustralis Incognita is an early-stage concept for a distributed Australian network spanning space, critical minerals and advanced technology. Port Hedland, Western Australia, is proposed as the first anchor node, with possible Phase 1 functions including propellant production, recovery and refurbishment infrastructure, and critical-minerals and battery processing. Before any of this could be considered realistic, it must be tested by an independent study.

## 3. Objectives of the Study

The study should determine, on the basis of evidence rather than aspiration, whether a Phase 1 hub at Port Hedland is technically achievable, environmentally acceptable, culturally appropriate, commercially viable and capable of being approved — and if so, at what scale, at what cost, and over what timeframe.

The study should be free, and expected, to conclude that the concept should not proceed, or should proceed only in a substantially changed form, if that is what the evidence shows.

## 4. Study Scope — Areas to be Examined

The study should address each of the following workstreams:

- Cultural heritage and Traditional Owner engagement — carried out with, and under the direction of, Traditional Owners: identification of cultural values, the engagement and consent process, and benefit-sharing and participation options. This workstream comes first by design.
- Technical and engineering feasibility — propellant production processes, storage, recovery and refurbishment infrastructure, site engineering, and the geotechnical and marine surveys required.
- Energy supply — the availability, reliability and cost of gas and electricity at the scale a Phase 1 hub would require.
- Environmental assessment — marine and coastal ecology, baseline studies, air and water quality, cumulative impact alongside existing port activity, and the environmental approvals pathway.
- Site, port and logistics — land availability, port capacity and integration with existing operations, transport links and supply chain.
- Regulatory and approvals pathway — identification of and compliance with all applicable Commonwealth and Western Australian law — including native title, Aboriginal cultural heritage, environmental protection, and space launch and return regulation — and a realistic mapping of the approvals timeline. The study should confirm the current applicable legislation in each area.
- Commercial and financial analysis — capital and operating cost estimates, funding and ownership models, market and demand analysis, and economic impact including regional employment.
- Risk assessment — technical, environmental, social, commercial and schedule risks, with realistic mitigation options.

## 5. Key Questions the Study Must Answer

- Can the region’s energy resources realistically and affordably supply propellant production at a useful scale?
- What are the genuine environmental constraints, and can they be met?
- Do Traditional Owners support the concept, and if so, on what terms?
- What would a Phase 1 hub actually cost to build and to operate?
- What approvals are required, and how long would they realistically take?
- Is there genuine demand for the propellant and processing capacity?
- What is the smallest viable first step — and is it worth taking?

## 6. Who Should Conduct the Study

The study should be carried out by independent, suitably qualified specialists — not by the concept’s proponents. Relevant disciplines include aerospace and process engineering; energy systems; environmental science and marine ecology; cultural heritage practitioners working under the direction of Traditional Owners; port and logistics planning; cost estimation and quantity surveying; economics; and regulatory and approvals expertise. An established engineering or infrastructure advisory firm would typically lead and coordinate the specialist input.

## 7. Governance and Traditional Owner Involvement

Traditional Owners should have a defined role in overseeing the study, not only in being consulted by it. The Port Hedland area is Kariyarra Country; the relevant native title and representative bodies should be engaged from the outset to agree how the study is conducted, what is examined, and how cultural knowledge is handled and protected. Sensitive cultural information remains the property of Traditional Owners and must not be recorded or shared without their consent.

## 8. Suggested Approach and Deliverables

A practical approach is two stages: first a short pre-feasibility scoping study, to confirm whether a full study is warranted and to refine its scope; then, if justified, a full feasibility study. Deliverables would typically include a written feasibility report setting out the evidence and costings, a clear recommendation, and supporting technical, environmental and engagement documentation.

## 9. An Honest Note on Possible Outcomes

A feasibility study earns its value precisely because its conclusion is not decided in advance. It may find the concept sound; it may find it sound only at a different scale or in a different form; or it may find it unviable. Each of those outcomes is genuinely useful — a clear “no” or “not like this” saves far more than the study costs. This scope is written in that spirit.

This is a scoping document to support the commissioning of an independent feasibility study. It does not itself assess or confirm feasibility.
