# The Incognita Maps

Four historical charts, spanning 1593 to 1814, that document the thing this
project is named for.

**Belt: Science.** These are real, published, dateable artefacts held in public
collections. Unlike every other image in `vision-plates/`, they are not
illustrations of a claim — they *are* the primary evidence, and they can be
checked against any map library.

They are also the strongest possible argument for the Incognita Rule, because
they show it happening in slow motion across 221 years: a continent drawn
confidently, then partially, then correctly, then named.

---

## 1593 — Cornelis de Jode, *Descriptio Terrae Subaustralis*

`1593-de-jode-descriptio-terrae-subaustralis.jpg`

From de Jode's *Speculum Orbis Terrae* (Antwerp). A vast southern continent
fills the polar projection, labelled **MAGALLANICA sive TERRA AUSTRALIS
INCOGNITA**, with named provinces — *Psittacorum Regio* (Region of Parrots),
*Beach Provincia*, *Promontorium terrae australis*.

**Nobody had been there.** The landmass is inferred, not surveyed: classical
geographers argued a great southern continent must exist to balance the
landmasses of the north, and cartographers drew that inference at full
confidence, with coastlines, capes and regional names. The parrots were
reported by hearsay from Marco Polo's *Beach*, itself a misreading.

This is the archetype the project's central rule was written against: a dreamed
line drawn in the same ink as a surveyed one, and indistinguishable to the
reader.

## 1658 — Nicolaas Visscher, *Orbis Terrarum Nova et Accuratissima Tabula*

`1658-visscher-orbis-terrarum.jpg`

A double-hemisphere world map from Amsterdam, framed by allegorical figures of
the elements and seasons. Along the southern margins of both hemispheres:
**AUSTRALIA INCOGNITA**. On the Australian coast, **HOLLANDIA NOVA** — New
Holland.

The interesting part is the inconsistency. Dutch voyages had by now returned
real coastline for the west and north, and Visscher draws it. But the great
southern continent is still there at the edges, because the inference had not
yet been retired. **Surveyed and dreamed lines sit on the same sheet, in the
same hand, with nothing to tell them apart.**

## 1663 — Melchisédech Thévenot, after Tasman

`1663-thevenot-hollandia-nova-terra-australis.jpg`

Published in *Relations de Divers Voyages Curieux* (Paris), derived from Abel
Tasman's voyages. The cartouche reads *"A Complete Map of the Southern
Continent… survey'd by Capt. Abel Jansen Tasman"*, and the coastline is dated
**1644**.

West, north and south coasts are real. Tasmania is there. **The entire east
coast is absent** — not guessed at, not filled in with plausible shoreline, but
simply left blank, with New Holland and Terra Australis lettered across open
paper.

This is the most honest map of the four, and the least complete. That is not a
coincidence, and it is the whole point: *the blank is the finding*. A
cartographer who leaves the unknown unknown has done the harder thing.

## 1814 — Matthew Flinders, *General Chart of Terra Australis or Australia*

`1814-flinders-general-chart-terra-australis.jpg`

From *A Voyage to Terra Australis*, showing "the parts explored between 1798 and
1803" by Flinders in HMS *Investigator*. The coastline closes. NEW HOLLAND is
lettered west, NEW SOUTH WALES east, VAN DIEMEN'S LAND south.

The title carries the argument. Flinders pressed for **Australia** over Terra
Australis, and this chart is where that name reaches print on an authoritative
survey. The Incognita is gone from the title because the incognita is gone from
the map — not by decree, but because someone sailed the whole way round and
wrote down what was there.

## Why these four are kept together

| Year | State of the claim | Stage 13 verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1593 | Inferred continent, drawn at full confidence | contradicted |
| 1658 | Real coast and imagined continent, same sheet | partly observed, partly contradicted |
| 1663 | Real coast, east coast left blank | observed, incomplete — and marked as such |
| 1814 | Circumnavigated and closed | observed |

Read left to right, this is the Blade Protocol's verdict scale applied to two
centuries of cartography — see `../BLADE-PROTOCOL.md`. The 1663 chart is the one
worth sitting with: it is the least impressive to look at and the most
trustworthy, because it declines to fill the gap.

> Always mark which lines are dreamed and which are surveyed, and never let a
> dreamed line pretend it was measured.

De Jode did not do this. Thévenot did. That is the entire distance the rule is
trying to cover.

## Provenance and rights

All four are reproductions of works published between 1593 and 1814 and long out
of copyright. The scans were supplied to this repository without source
attribution; **a reproduction can carry its own rights in some jurisdictions**,
so if any of these are to be published or printed, trace the specific scan to
its holding institution first. Likely holders include the National Library of
Australia, the State Library of NSW, and the David Rumsey Map Collection.

Dates and attributions here are from the cartographic record and the maps' own
lettering. Where a chart is a derivative — Thévenot's is after Tasman, and
survives in several states — the date given is the publication, not the survey.
